About JordanWatch

Rates lakes Avoid, Caution or Low Risk for harmful algal blooms.

  • For swimmers, paddlers, dog owners
  • Built for Jordan Lake, NC
  • About 7,000 US lakes
  • Free · No account · No ads

How it works

  • Satellite EPA CyAN cells/mL vs. WHO 1999/2003 levels
    More

    EPA's CyAN project estimates cyanobacteria (the algae behind harmful blooms) from satellite images, in cells per milliliter, with pixels about 300 m wide. Under 20,000 cells/mL is Low Risk, 20,000 to 100,000 is Caution, and over 100,000 is Avoid.

  • Cells, not toxins Satellites can't measure toxins
    More

    NC DHHS and EPA judge recreation by a toxin, microcystin (8 µg/L), which needs a water test. Satellites can't measure toxins, so JordanWatch uses WHO's cell-count levels as an early warning.

  • Other signals Rain, rivers, lake level, wind
    More

    Heavy rain in the last 48 hours or a river running higher than usual can wash bacteria into the water, so they can raise a verdict to Caution. So can a lake well above its usual level. Light wind can pile floating algae on the downwind shore, so beaches there go up one level.

  • Worst signal wins Old data never gives an all-clear
    More

    Each verdict is the worst of its signals, and the Why card shows every one of them. A reading over a week old can keep a warning but can't give Low Risk.

  • Camera scan A screening aid, not a diagnosis
    More

    A model trained on photos of blooms, plants, clear water and shorelines looks at what your camera sees. It says Uncertain when it isn't sure, and a bloom you confirm near one of Jordan Lake's areas raises that area's verdict, on your phone only.

About me

My name is Svar, and I'm a high school student at Green Level High School in Cary, North Carolina. I built JordanWatch because harmful algal blooms are an unfortunate reality in our local lakes. It's important to know when a lake is risky before you get in, so that people and pets can avoid harmful exposure.

JordanWatch combines public satellite data with U.S. Geological Survey stations to rate about 7,000 lakes across the United States. In doing so, data is collected and organized into a format easily readable by the public.

How to use JordanWatch · 1 of 6

What JordanWatch does

JordanWatch tells you whether a lake is likely to have a harmful algal bloom today: the green, paint-like scum that can make people and dogs sick.

It checks about 7,000 US lakes with EPA satellite images, USGS sensors, rain and wind, and gives each lake one rating:

  • Avoid
  • Caution
  • Low Risk
  1. Today's rating. Avoid means stay out of the water. Caution means no swimming, but boating and fishing are OK. Low Risk means the satellite saw no bloom.

It was built for Jordan Lake in North Carolina, where it rates 10 separate areas, and it works the same way for lakes across the country.

Find your lake

The app opens on Jordan Lake as an example. To see the lakes near you:

  1. The place button. Tap the place name at the top of the screen to choose where you are.
  2. Search. Type a 5-digit ZIP code or part of a lake's name, like "falls" or "tahoe". Matching lakes appear as you type; tap one to open it.
  3. Use my location. Or let the app use your phone's location. Your exact location stays on your phone.

Read the answer

Every lake uses the same layout, from top to bottom:

  1. The rating. Avoid, Caution or Low Risk, with the date of the newest satellite image and whether it's getting better or worse.
  2. What to do today. Short tips for people, kids and dogs: whether swimming, wading or boating is OK, and to rinse off with tap water after contact.
  3. A better spot nearby. If the lake is at Avoid or Caution, the app suggests the nearest lake (at Jordan Lake, the nearest area) with a better rating. Tap it to switch.
  4. Why. One small card per signal: satellite algae, rain and river flow, lake level and wind. The worst signal sets the rating. Tap Details for the exact numbers, and see the 30-day trend chart below.

The map and more

Scroll down to the satellite map and the extra cards:

  1. Save a lake. Tap the star on any lake or area. Saved lakes appear in a "Your lakes" row at the top next time.
  2. Satellite colors. Where the satellite saw algae in the past week, from light yellow (low) to red and purple (very high). No color means clean water, clouds or no satellite pass.
  3. Rating pins. Every lake has a pin in its rating's color; gray means no recent reading. Tap a pin to open that lake, or tap Full map for a bigger view.

Further down, Your tap water shows which lake your drinking water likely comes from (context only, not a tap-water rating), and Live stations shows nearby USGS water sensors, each judged by its own state's standards.

Right at the shore

Satellites can't see the shoreline or small ponds, but your phone can.

  1. The Scan tab. The round camera button in the bottom bar.
  2. Scan the water. Tap Start camera, point at the water near the shore, then tap Scan. The model says possible bloom, plants, clear water or not water, and how sure it is. You can also check a photo you already took.
  3. If exposed. If you, a child or a pet touched bloom water, this red button shows what to do and the numbers to call.

Seeing a bloom? Use Report a bloom (in What to do) to send it to your state's environmental agency. The camera is a screening aid, not a diagnosis.

Good to know

  • Updated automatically. New satellite data arrives several times a day. Satellite images are usually 1 to 3 days old, and the app always shows their date.
  • Old data never gives an all-clear. A reading over a week old can keep a warning, but it can't make a lake Low Risk.
  • Cells, not toxins. Satellites count algae cells; they can't measure toxins. The ratings use the World Health Organization's cell-count levels as an early warning.
  • Blind spots. Narrow arms, the shoreline and lakes smaller than about a square kilometer are too small for the satellite, and clouds hide lakes some days. That's what the camera scan is for.
  • More tab. How it works, data sources and About.
  • Not an official advisory. Always follow posted signs and your state's advisories.
